BEFORE YOU TRY · TWO GUIDED EXAMPLES

Let's connect two facts step by step

Watch how two short sentences become one sentence with a defining relative clause.

EXAMPLE 1 · DESCRIBING A PERSON

FACT A: Alexander Graham Bell was an inventor.

FACT B: He developed an early telephone.

STEP 1 · Find the repeated idea: Alexander Graham Bell = he. Both facts talk about the same person.

STEP 2 · Choose the connector: Use WHO because we are describing a person.

STEP 3 · Join the ideas:

Alexander Graham Bell was an inventor who developed an early telephone.

Notice: The clause who developed an early telephone tells us which inventor we mean. We do not repeat his name or use he again.

EXAMPLE 2 · DESCRIBING A THING

FACT A: The telephone is a communication device.

FACT B: People use this device to talk to someone far away.

STEP 1 · Find the repeated idea: the telephone = this device. Both facts talk about the same thing.

STEP 2 · Choose the connector: Use THAT or WHICH because we are describing a thing.

STEP 3 · Join the ideas:

The telephone is a communication device that people use to talk to someone far away.

Notice: The clause that people use to talk to someone far away gives us more information about the device. The sentence is now more connected and less repetitive.

GRAMMAR DISCOVERY

What is a defining relative clause?

A defining relative clause is a part of a sentence that gives essential information about a person, thing, place or time. It tells the reader exactly who or what we mean.

Start with two simple sentences

SENTENCE A

A tactile reading system uses patterns of raised dots.

SENTENCE B

The system can be read by touch.

Both sentences talk about the same person. We can join them and avoid repeating the name.
ONE CONNECTED SENTENCE

A tactile reading system that uses patterns of raised dots can be read by touch.

The relative clause tells us which reading system we mean. It is essential information, so this is a defining relative clause.

Find the noun

The noun is the person, thing, place or time we want to describe.

The inventor who developed the telephone...
A device that helps doctors listen...

Find the relative clause

It begins with a relative word and tells us more about the noun.

The inventor who developed the telephone...
A device that helps doctors listen...
THE BIG IDEA

Think of it like a label attached to a noun

A relative clause works like a detailed label. It answers “Which one?” or “What kind?” and helps the reader identify the noun more precisely.

The invention that changed communication became popular around the world.
Which invention? The one that changed communication.
GRAMMAR TOOLKIT · STEP BY STEP

Choose the right relative word

First find the noun you want to describe. Then ask: Is it a person, a thing, a place, a time, or does it show possession?

Relative wordUse it for…Example connected to inventionsAsk yourself…
WHOPeopleLouis Braille was the educator who developed the system.Am I describing a person?
WHICHThings / animalsThe stethoscope is an instrument which helps doctors listen.Am I describing a thing?
THATPeople or things in defining clausesThe device that changed communication was the telephone.Can I use a general option for a person or thing?
WHEREPlacesThe laboratory where he worked had special equipment.Am I describing a place?
WHENTimes / moments1903 was the year when the Wright brothers flew.Am I describing a time?
WHOSEPossession / relationshipLaennec was the doctor whose invention helped medicine.Does something belong to this person / thing?
Important: “That” can replace “who” or “which” in defining relative clauses. It is common in everyday English. Do not use “that” after a comma in a non-defining clause. Today, focus on defining clauses.

Build a sentence: where does the clause go?

FORMULA

NOUN + RELATIVE WORD + CLAUSE

The inventor + who + developed the telephone

The relative clause comes immediately after the noun it describes.

FULL SENTENCE

Put the parts together

The inventor who developed the telephone changed communication.

Do not put the clause at the end if that makes it unclear which noun it describes.

Subject or object? This is the part that often causes confusion.

A relative word can do two different jobs inside the relative clause. Let's look at the difference.

THE RELATIVE WORD DOES THE ACTION

Subject relative clause

The inventor who created the device became famous.

The inventor created the device. The word who replaces “the inventor” and does the action (created).

Do not remove the relative word.
❌ The inventor created the device became famous.
✅ The inventor who created the device became famous.

Look after the relative word: it is followed directly by a verb (created).

THE RELATIVE WORD RECEIVES THE ACTION

Object relative clause

The device that the inventor created became famous.

The inventor created the device. Inside the relative clause, “the inventor” is the subject and “that” represents the device (the object).

You can remove the relative word.
✅ The device that the inventor created became famous.
✅ The device the inventor created became famous.

Look after the relative word: you see a subject (the inventor) before the verb (created).

A quick way to recognize the difference

WHO + VERB

The scientist who discovered the problem...

WHO does the action. Do not omit it.

WHO / THAT + SUBJECT + VERB

The problem that the scientist discovered...

The scientist does the action. You may omit THAT.

Defining means essential: no commas

NOT DEFINING · NOT TODAY'S FOCUS

The telephone, which was invented in the 19th century, changed communication.

The clause is extra information; commas separate it.

DEFINING · TODAY'S FOCUS

The invention that changed communication was the telephone.

The clause tells us which invention. No commas.

Remember: In a defining relative clause, the information helps identify the noun. We do not put commas around it.
